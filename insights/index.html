BUYING GUIDE6 MIN READ

How to write a sourcing brief suppliers can quote accurately

A useful brief gives suppliers enough context to identify a suitable option and gives your team a fair basis for comparing responses.

Start with the job the product needs to do. State the intended application, operating environment and delivery destination. A reference image can help explain the request, but it rarely captures dimensions, materials, performance or what must be included in the supply.

Include these eight details

  1. Product and application: describe what is needed and where it will be used.
  2. Specification: attach a drawing, data sheet, model reference, material, finish or required dimensions, and identify the current revision.
  3. Quantity and unit: distinguish initial quantity from likely repeat demand, and state pack or set requirements.
  4. Must-haves and flexibility: separate essential requirements from preferences; say whether alternatives may be proposed.
  5. Evidence required: name the product documents, samples or checks your review process needs. Ask for item-specific evidence where applicable.
  6. Scope of supply: identify accessories, assembly, packaging, labelling or other inclusions that affect comparison.
  7. Destination and timing: give the delivery location and target decision or readiness date, noting site or access constraints.
  8. Decision process: identify who can answer technical questions and what information is needed for internal approval.

Ask suppliers to flag assumptions and exclusions instead of silently filling gaps. Keep open questions visible in the comparison so the team can resolve them before an order is placed. For regulated or safety-critical applications, the buyer remains responsible for defining and approving the applicable requirements; a general product description does not establish suitability.

Useful next step: send one item schedule with a clear reference for every line, then invite suppliers to respond against the same specification and delivery scope.

PROCUREMENT5 MIN READ

How to compare supplier quotations like for like

A lower unit price is only meaningful when the items, quantities and included services are comparable.

Before reviewing totals, confirm that each quotation refers to the same item description, drawing revision, quantity and unit of measure. Similar names or photographs do not prove that specifications match. Record any proposed substitution separately and route it to the person authorised to assess technical suitability.

Build a comparison around the full scope

  • Product: model or reference, dimensions, material, finish, options and specification revision.
  • Quantity basis: unit, set or carton; minimum order quantity; price breaks; and any tooling or sample charges.
  • Inclusions: accessories, packaging, labelling, assembly, inspection or documents that are included or excluded.
  • Delivery: quoted shipping basis, destination, freight assumptions, readiness estimate and any local handling requirements.
  • Commercial terms: validity period, payment terms, currency, taxes or charges stated in the offer, and conditions that still need agreement.
  • Open items: unanswered questions, unconfirmed alternatives and evidence still required for your review.

Separate confirmed facts from estimates and assumptions. If an offer excludes freight while another includes delivery, show the difference rather than treating the headline totals as equivalent. Lead times should also be read in context: production readiness, collection, transit and site delivery are different milestones and each may need confirmation.

Use a short decision record to capture the selected offer, the reason it meets the requirement, outstanding conditions and the person approving any deviation. This creates a useful reference for purchasing, supplier coordination and later delivery checks.

Useful next step: ask every supplier to confirm exceptions against your item schedule before you finalise the comparison.

QUALITY PLANNING5 MIN READ

Plan quality checkpoints before production begins

A practical inspection plan begins with the approved order requirements and identifies what evidence is needed at each agreed review point.

Quality checks are most useful when they are connected to a specific product, order and acceptance basis. Before production, align the purchase description, quantity, drawings, approved sample or reference, finish and required documents. Record revisions so the supplier and buyer are working from the same version.

Agree the checkpoints in advance

  1. Requirement review: confirm the order references the approved specification and identifies any critical characteristics.
  2. Pre-production alignment: resolve material, colour, dimensions, packaging and labelling questions before work proceeds.
  3. In-process review, if needed: decide whether an agreed progress check is appropriate for the order and what it will cover.
  4. Pre-shipment review: define the sample basis, visual or dimensional checks, quantity reconciliation and documents to be reviewed.
  5. Release decision: identify who reviews the findings, how exceptions are recorded and what approval is needed before dispatch.

The depth of review should fit the product and project risk. A visual check cannot establish every material property or regulatory requirement. Where a test, certification or specialist assessment is required, specify the relevant method and evidence and confirm who will arrange it. Do not treat a general certificate as proof that a particular supplied item meets every intended use.

Keep dated records tied to the item or lot: the reference inspected, quantity checked, observations, supporting photographs or reports, exceptions and agreed disposition. If a deviation is proposed, pause the affected decision until the authorised buyer has reviewed it.

Useful next step: add the quality checkpoints, responsible reviewer and required records to the order plan before production starts.
